      How to calculate your freelance hourly rate

      Most new freelancers set their rate by looking at what others charge or by dividing an old salary by 2,080 hours. Both approaches quietly lose money. A sustainable rate works backwards from what you need to earn, and this calculator follows that logic step by step:

      1. Start with your take-home goal. This is what you want to actually keep each year — the equivalent of a net salary.
      2. Gross it up for taxes. Divide the take-home goal by (1 − tax rate). At a 25% effective rate, keeping $60,000 requires $80,000 of pre-tax profit.
      3. Add business expenses. Software subscriptions, hardware, insurance, coworking, accountant fees, marketing, courses. These are paid by your business before you see a cent.
      4. Add a buffer. A 10–20% margin covers slow months, late payments and unplanned costs. Without it, one quiet quarter puts you behind for the year.
      5. Count your billable hours honestly. Billable hours per day × working days per week × (52 − weeks off). Five billable hours a day, five days a week and six weeks off is 1,150 hours — not 2,080.
      6. Divide. Gross revenue needed ÷ billable hours = your minimum hourly rate. Everything above it is profit; anything below means you are subsidising your clients.

      The breakdown bar shows how each dollar of revenue is split between take-home, taxes, expenses and buffer, which is a useful reality check when a rate feels "too high". Usually only 55–70% of what you invoice ends up in your pocket.

      Common mistakes when setting a freelance rate

      Copying your old employee salary

      An employee's hourly equivalent hides a lot: employer-paid taxes and benefits, paid holidays, equipment, training and a steady 40 billable hours a week. As a freelancer you fund all of that yourself, so matching your old hourly figure typically means a 30–50% pay cut in real terms.

      Forgetting non-billable time

      Prospecting, proposals, calls, invoicing, bookkeeping and learning new tools easily consume 30–40% of a working week. If you price as though every hour is billable, your effective income is far lower than your rate suggests. The calculator shows your derived non-billable share so you can see this plainly.

      Ignoring taxes until the bill arrives

      Self-employed people pay both the employee and employer side of social contributions in most countries, plus income tax, often in quarterly estimates. Build your effective rate into the price from day one and set the money aside as you invoice.

      Pricing without a buffer

      Clients pay late, projects pause, laptops die. A buffer margin is not greed; it is the difference between a business and a hobby.

      Hourly vs project vs retainer pricing

      Hourly is transparent and protects you when scope is unclear, but it caps your income at hours worked and penalises efficiency. Project (fixed) pricing is easier for clients to say yes to and lets you earn more as you get faster, provided you scope carefully and price in revision rounds. Retainers — a fixed monthly fee for ongoing availability or a block of hours — smooth your cash flow and are usually priced with a small discount in exchange for commitment. Many freelancers use all three: hourly for discovery and maintenance, fixed fees for defined deliverables, retainers for long-term clients. Whatever the model, your hourly floor from this calculator is the number no quote should fall below.

      How to raise rates with existing clients

      • Give notice. 30–60 days is standard; announce the new rate for work starting after a specific date.
      • Lead with value, not costs. Mention results delivered and expanded skills rather than your rent going up.
      • Keep it short and confident. A two-paragraph email beats a long justification. Most clients expect periodic increases.
      • Offer a path. A retainer or prepaid block at a slightly lower rate makes the increase easier to accept.
      • Raise with new clients first. Test the new rate on fresh proposals, then bring existing clients up to it.

      A note on benchmarks

      Published "average freelance rates" vary enormously by country, specialty, seniority and how the survey was run, so treat them as context, not a target. This calculator does not use benchmark data; it computes the rate your numbers require. If the result is far above what your market pays, the fix is usually to raise billable efficiency, lower expenses, specialise, or target clients with bigger budgets — not to accept a rate that cannot sustain you.

      Frequently asked questions

      How does the freelance hourly rate calculator work?

      It starts from the take-home income you want, adds the taxes you will owe on it, adds your annual business expenses and a profit buffer, then divides that gross revenue by the number of hours you can realistically bill in a year (billable hours per day × working days per week × working weeks). The result is the minimum hourly rate that gets you to your goal.

      What is a good freelance hourly rate?

      There is no universal number. A good rate is one that covers your target income, taxes, expenses and unpaid time, and that your market will pay. As a rough sanity check, many freelancers land between 1.5× and 3× the hourly equivalent of a comparable salaried job, because they must cover benefits, downtime and overhead themselves. Use the calculator for your floor, then adjust upward for experience, specialisation and demand.

      Should I charge hourly or per project?

      Hourly billing is simplest for open-ended or poorly defined work. Fixed project pricing rewards you for being efficient and is easier for clients to approve, but you carry the risk of underestimating. Many freelancers compute a fixed price from estimated hours × hourly rate, then add a margin for value and risk. The project quote helper in this tool does exactly that.

      How many hours a year can a freelancer actually bill?

      Far fewer than 2,080. Between finding clients, admin, invoicing, learning and gaps between projects, most full-time freelancers bill 1,000 to 1,400 hours per year — roughly 4 to 6 billable hours per working day. The calculator defaults to 5 billable hours a day, 5 days a week and 6 weeks off, which comes to 1,150 billable hours.

      Do I need to include taxes in my hourly rate?

      Yes. As a freelancer nobody withholds tax for you, so your rate must cover income tax, self-employment or social contributions and any local business taxes. Enter your effective (blended) tax rate as a percentage and the calculator grosses up your income so that what is left after tax matches your goal.
